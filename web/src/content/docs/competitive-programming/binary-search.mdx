Binary search is a four-line loop with two well-known ways of going wrong, and
neither one announces itself. The code compiles either way. One version answers
a slightly different question and is off by one index. The other never answers
at all, because the loop stops getting smaller.

The program below prints every step it takes: the two indices, the middle value
it looked at, and the part of the array still in play.

## The window is the whole idea

<SnippetSource name="23-competitive.binary-search" decl="lowerBound" />

`lo` and `hi` are not the ends of the array. They are the ends of the part
still worth looking at, and the range is half-open, so the live window is
`items[lo..hi]` and `items[hi]` sits outside it.

One sentence has to stay true from the first pass to the last: every index that
could be the answer is inside the window. `items[mid] < key` proves `mid` is
too small, so `lo = mid + 1` discards `mid` along with everything below it. The
other branch proves only that `mid` is not too small, which leaves it a
candidate, so `hi = mid` keeps it. Lose that asymmetry and the search can throw
away the index it was standing on.

When `lo` reaches `hi` the window holds nothing. Nothing was discarded that
could have been the answer, so `lo` is the answer, and there is no separate
not-found case to write:

```
  step 1  lo= 0 mid= 6 hi=12    1   3   3   4   7   7[ 7]   9  11  11  15  20
  step 2  lo= 0 mid= 3 hi= 6    1   3   3[ 4]   7   7   .   .   .   .   .   .
  step 3  lo= 4 mid= 5 hi= 6    .   .   .   .   7[ 7]   .   .   .   .   .   .
  step 4  lo= 4 mid= 4 hi= 5    .   .   .   .[ 7]   .   .   .   .   .   .   .
  window empty after 4 steps: lo = hi = 4
```

Twelve values, four passes. The dots are the indices already ruled out, and
each step rules out about half of what remains.

`lo + (hi - lo) / 2` is the midpoint written the long way. `(lo + hi) / 2` reads
better and adds two indices that are each valid on their own, on the assumption
that the sum is too. Java's `Arrays.binarySearch` shipped that version for years
before the overflow was written up in 2006. A 64-bit `usize` will not get you
there, a 32-bit target with a large array will, and the long form costs one
subtraction.

## The off-by-one does not give a wrong answer

<SnippetSource name="23-competitive.binary-search" decl="stalledLowerBound" />

Write `lo = mid` instead of `lo = mid + 1` and the search stops ending. The
thinking behind it holds up for a moment. `mid` was too small, and leaving it
in the window looks like it costs one extra comparison.

It costs the loop instead. Once `hi - lo` is 1, integer division puts `mid` at
`lo`, and assigning `lo = mid` leaves both indices where they were:

```
  step 3  lo= 3 mid= 4 hi= 6    .   .   .   4[ 7]   7   .   .   .   .   .   .
  step 4  lo= 3 mid= 3 hi= 4    .   .   .[ 4]   .   .   .   .   .   .   .   .
  step 5  lo= 3 mid= 3 hi= 4    .   .   .[ 4]   .   .   .   .   .   .   .   .
  step 6  lo= 3 mid= 3 hi= 4    .   .   .[ 4]   .   .   .   .   .   .   .   .
  no progress: gave up at step 6 with lo=3 hi=4
```

Steps four, five and six are the same step. The step budget is what lets the
page print this at all, since the real version runs until something kills it.
On a judge that something is the time limit, and the verdict names the clock
rather than the line, so this one is expensive to find.

One check catches this before it reaches a judge. The window has to be strictly
smaller after every pass. Both branches shrink it by at least one index here,
and there is no third branch that could leave it alone.

## Where a value starts, and where it ends

<SnippetSource name="23-competitive.binary-search" decl="upperBound" />

One character separates the two bounds. `<` stops at the first value that is
not smaller than the key. `<=` walks past every value equal to it. So
`lowerBound` returns the first index holding the key and `upperBound` returns
the first index past it, and the pair is a half-open range in the same style as
the window that found them.

That range answers the question a problem usually asks: how many, not where.

```
  key  lower  upper  count  present
    7      4      7      3  yes
    5      4      4      0  no
   20     11     12      1  yes
    0      0      0      0  no
   21     12     12      0  no
```

Look at the rows for 5 and 21. When the two bounds agree the key is absent, and
the index they agree on is where it would go if you inserted it. Sorting a
handful of new values into a sorted array uses that, and so does counting the
values below a threshold, where `lowerBound` alone is the answer.

## What the standard library already has

Both bounds ship in `std.sort`, along with `equalRange` for the pair and
`binarySearch` for plain membership. None of them needs the loop above written
out again. They do need a comparator, and its argument order catches people
out:

<SnippetSource name="23-competitive.binary-search" decl="orderKeyFirst" />

The key comes first and the element second. Swap them and the code still
compiles, because both arguments are the same type, and the answer comes back
mirrored. On the array in this chapter:

```
  std.sort.lowerBound(7) = 4, ours = 4
  std.sort.upperBound(7) = 7, ours = 7
  std.sort.equalRange(7) = .{ 4, 7 }
  std.sort.binarySearch(5) = null, lowerBound(5) = 4
```

`binarySearch` returns `?usize` and gives back some matching index rather than
the first, so reach for `lowerBound` whenever duplicates are possible. The last
row shows the difference for an absent key: `binarySearch` says null, and
`lowerBound` says 4, which is the position 5 would occupy.

All of this assumes the array is already sorted, and nothing in `std.sort`
checks that for you. [Sorting](/learn/standard-library/sorting/) covers
`std.mem.sort`, the stable and unstable variants, and the context argument
these comparators share.

## The input is a const, because there is no stdin

<SnippetSource name="23-competitive.binary-search" decl="readRow" />

A snippet on this site runs under WASI in a browser tab, which has no standard
input to read. The input therefore lives in the file as a string, and the
parser takes a `*std.Io.Reader` over it rather than the string itself.

The indirection is not a workaround. A function that turns bytes into values
runs unchanged over a file, over a socket and over a literal, which is the same
reason the networking chapters parse protocols from a reader instead of from a
connection. Point `readRow` at stdin on a judge and not a line of it changes.
[Readers and Writers](/learn/standard-library/readers-and-writers/) covers the
interface and the buffer it reads through.
