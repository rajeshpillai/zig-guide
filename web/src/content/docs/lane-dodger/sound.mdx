There are no audio files in this project. The five effects are arithmetic,
generated when the game starts.

That is a deliberate trade and worth stating before the code. Recording or
licensing five blips is faster than writing a synthesiser. What it costs is a
folder of binaries nobody can diff, a licence question attached to each one, and
a tuning loop that runs through an audio editor. Six hundred lines of arithmetic
gives sounds you change by changing a number, and keeps the whole game a text
tree, which is the same reason the graphics here are shapes rather than sprites.

## One oscillator with an envelope

Everything is built from stacking these.

<GameSource file="src/audio/synth.zig" decl="Layer" />

A frequency sweep from `from` to `to`, an amplitude envelope, and an optional
low pass to take the fizz off noise. `delay` staggers a layer, which is how the
arpeggios are made without any sequencing code.

The coin is two layers, a triangle and a quieter sine an octave above it:

<GameSource file="src/audio/synth.zig" decl="coin" />

The crash is six, and the last three are the falling triad that plays over the
game over screen:

<GameSource file="src/audio/synth.zig" decl="crash" />

That is one sound rather than two, and it is the more interesting decision. The
alternative is playing the thud on impact and the sting when the retry prompt
appears, which means the game has to remember whether it has played the sting
yet, for a run it has already lost. Layers take a delay, so the pause lives in
the waveform instead and no state is needed.

## The renderer

<GameSource file="src/audio/synth.zig" decl="render" />

The sweep is exponential rather than linear, so a slide from 900 Hz to 1350 Hz
is even in pitch rather than even in hertz. Pitch is logarithmic and a linear
sweep sounds like it accelerates.

Layers are summed and then soft clipped with `tanh` rather than clamped. Stacked
layers overshoot, and clamping shears the peaks flat, which is audible as
distortion. `tanh` bends them.

## The envelope, and the click

<GameSource file="src/audio/synth.zig" decl="envelope" />

The envelope reaches exactly zero at both ends of every layer, and this is the
detail most worth taking from this chapter.

A clip that begins at full amplitude begins with a step from silence to whatever
the waveform happened to be at. A step is a broadband impulse. It is a click,
and it is louder and more irritating than the effect it is attached to. The same
is true at the end. Four milliseconds of fade in is enough, and the decay is
shaped so that it lands on zero rather than being cut off there.

This is the sort of thing that is obvious in one second of listening and
invisible in a diff, which makes it a good thing to test.

## Testing a waveform

The synthesiser holds no raylib types. It fills a buffer with samples and stops.
So the sounds are checked without an audio device, which is just as well,
because CI has no speakers.

<GameSource file="src/audio/synth.zig" decl="no sound begins or ends with a click" />

Then the mix. Absolute frequencies are a matter of taste and are allowed to
move. What must not move is the arrangement: the crash is the low long loud one,
and the lane tick, which fires on every single input, must not shout over a
pickup.

<GameSource file="src/audio/synth.zig" decl="the sounds sit in the right places against each other" />

Brightness there is zero crossings per second, a crude pitch estimate that is
more than good enough to tell a chime from a thud.

Measured, the five come out as intended: the coin at about 1130 Hz over 85 ms,
the crash 900 ms long with 81 percent of its energy below 500 Hz, the lane tick
shortest and quietest. `zig build sounds` writes them all out as `.wav` so they
can be listened to without playing the game up to the point that triggers each
one.

## Optional at runtime, on purpose

A machine with no sound card, a container with no ALSA, a browser tab nobody has
clicked in yet: all of those leave the device unopened, and every call above
turns into nothing.

<GameSource file="src/platform/audio.zig" decl="init" />

A game that refuses to start because it could not open a mixer is a worse game
than a silent one. This is checked by running the real binary with a deliberately
broken ALSA configuration and asserting it still exits cleanly.

## What this chapter is really about

Sound was added to this game after it was finished and playing well. It changed
no rule, and it could not have, because it reads the same
[event stream](/learn/lane-dodger/entities/) the particles read and the
simulation cannot see it.

That is the payoff for the separation the
[first chapter](/learn/lane-dodger/what-we-are-building/) described. The
fifty-five tests were all still green afterwards, and not one of them needed a
speaker.
